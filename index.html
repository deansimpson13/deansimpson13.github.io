AM transmitter and receiver

TransmitterXR2206 function generator kit, modulated through its AMSI pin
ReceiverHand-wound coil, adjustable capacitor, 1N34 diode, LM386 amplifier
Tuningf = 1 / (2π√LC)
Test gearOscilloscope, two function generators, multimeter, capacitance meter
When2025, team of three

Three of us built both halves of an AM link. The transmitter started from an XR2206 function generator kit. We traced a dead output on the scope to bad solder joints, then fed a second function generator into the AMSI pin to modulate the amplitude. Tuned to an open frequency, it came through on a regular AM radio.

The receiver only works if its LC circuit hits the right frequency, so we needed the coil's inductance. We found it two ways: from the coil's dimensions and number of turns, and from an LR decay on the scope. For the second, we drove the coil with a square wave, read the time constant at 37% of the peak, and solved L = τR. The two values agreed.

What worked
The transmitter came through on a regular AM radio from more than 15 feet away, and the receiver pulled in faint audio from a local station. A longer, higher antenna, the amplifier and a solid ground all made a clear difference.
What didn't
Our receiver never picked up our own transmitter, and its audio stayed quiet and full of static.
Next time
Measure L and C before building anything, and add the amplifier from the start.

ITCH 5.0 parser in Verilog

ProtocolNASDAQ TotalView-ITCH 5.0
WhenFall 2026, personal project
LanguageVerilog, checked against a Python reference model
Decodes9 message types (S R A F E C X D U); skips the other 14 cleanly
Tested on500,000 real NASDAQ messages, 0 mismatches
Synthesized forLattice iCE40-HX8K, about 91 MHz
StageSynthesized, not yet running on a board
Codeitch5-verilog-parser

ITCH is the binary feed NASDAQ uses to broadcast every order, cancel and trade. Trading firms parse it in FPGAs because hardware can decode a message in nanoseconds. I built a small version of that parser in Verilog, and spent most of my time checking that it decodes correctly.

Every message the hardware decodes gets compared field by field against a Python version. Both came from my reading of the spec, so they could share the same mistake. To catch that, I also ran an outside open-source parser on a slice of real NASDAQ data from January 2019. Over 500,000 real messages, the Verilog matched the Python model line for line, and the outside parser agreed on all 2.7 million fields.

I then synthesized it with free tools (Yosys and nextpnr) for a Lattice iCE40. The parser on its own takes 331 LUTs and 1,040 flip-flops, a small slice of the chip, and runs at about 91 MHz in the worst case. Shrinking one counter from 16 bits to 6 bits took that up from 81 MHz.

What testing caught
The decoder's buffer was one byte too short for the 40-byte F message. The Python comparison flagged it as soon as I added that type.
Limits
At one byte per clock it moves about 0.73 Gb/s, roughly 7% of 10 Gb Ethernet. Fine for testing, but far too slow for a live feed.
Next
Run it on a real FPGA board with ITCH bytes sent over UART, then build an order book on top of the decoded messages.

Multi-channel power logger

Parts ordered, sensor bring-up underway

A reusable bench tool built on an ESP32-S3 and two INA228 power monitors. It measures voltage, current and power on a device's input and output at the same time, and logs timestamped readings to CSV over USB, or to a microSD card for standalone runs. Each INA228 reads current from the drop across a 15 mΩ shunt and reports to the ESP32 over I2C. I'll check its accuracy against a multimeter on a known resistive load.

I'm building it as one shared core for a series of tests:

  • Efficiency of a discrete vs. an integrated 12 V to 5 V buck converter across load levels, with scope captures of the switching
  • Capacity and internal resistance of 18650 cells through a MOSFET-switched discharge load, then sorting them into matched groups
  • Power-on current, to tell normal boots apart from failures

Self-balancing robot

Chassis version 1, fall 2026

A two-wheeled robot that balances upright, holds its position and drives on command, with the electronics and chassis designed from scratch. An ESP32 combines an MPU6050 IMU with wheel encoders to run two PID loops: one keeps it upright, the other stops it from drifting. It runs on a protected 3S Li-ion pack and streams live data over WiFi for tuning.

The chassis is 3D printed. Version 2 will move to a stiffer frame and a custom PCB.
